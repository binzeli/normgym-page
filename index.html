Abstract

Normative competence, the ability to infer socially enforced expectations from interaction and adjust behavior accordingly, is essential for agents operating in unfamiliar human communities. Yet existing evaluations largely test whether agents can recognize or apply norms they may already know, rather than whether they can acquire previously unknown norms from social interaction. We introduce NormGym, an interactive benchmark for normative competence in which a newcomer must infer a hidden, arbitrary norm while pursuing its own task in a multi-agent community. Success requires distinguishing what is possible from what is permitted, inferring the norm from observed behavior and sanctions, and applying it in new situations. NormGym contains diverse norms and distinct environments, allowing systematic evaluation of current agents, including large language model (LLM) agents. We find that normative competence remains challenging: the strongest evaluated LLM achieves a joint task-and-compliance success rate of 55.3%. Our analysis reveals that models can sometimes revise their behavior following sanctions for their own actions, but struggle to use other agents’ behavior and the sanctions they receive to infer the underlying norm. We further find that fine-tuning models may be insufficient to improve normative competence. These results suggest that current models lack a robust, transferable capability for acquiring normative knowledge through social interaction.

Learning the local rules

In NormGym, a newcomer enters a community (a café, a post office, a grocery store) with a hidden norm. Breaking it is possible but publicly sanctioned, so the newcomer must work out the norm while doing its own task.

Arbitrary norms

If the norms were familiar ones, a language model could simply recall them from pretraining. So every norm in NormGym is deliberately arbitrary: a “silly rule” that a community follows only because it agrees to, much like human customs such as how far to stand from a stranger. Such rules serve no practical purpose, yet they are enforced just as seriously. The 50 norms fall into four families:

  • Access (12): where an agent may go, e.g. sit only in one area or avoid a zone.
  • Procedure (11): how or when an agent moves, e.g. move only on even time steps.
  • Coordination (16): position relative to others, e.g. join a staircase-shaped queue at its tail.
  • Allocation (11): how a shared resource is divided, e.g. at most two agents in a zone.

Results

1.The tasks are easy. The norms are not.

Claude Opus 5 completes its task 95.7% of the time but succeeds jointly only 55.3% of the time. When GPT-5.6 Sol is told the norm, its joint success jumps from 44.0% to 90.7%. The models can follow a norm once they know it; the hard part is figuring it out. Smaller open models almost never succeed, and per-norm DQN agents do little better.

2.Learning stalls early.

For the stronger models, belief correctness rises quickly over the first 50 steps and then levels off below the ground truth (panel b below). This is not for lack of evidence: they keep getting sanctioned for the rest of the episode. They settle on an incomplete belief and stop revising it.

3.Models learn from their own mistakes, not from others’.

Beliefs improve most after the model’s own violation, much less after watching another agent get sanctioned, and hardly at all after watching compliant behavior (panel c). Humans are the opposite: most say they learned mainly by watching others (panel d). In the example below, GPT-5.6 Sol keeps a wrong hypothesis through 173 observed moves that directly contradict it, and only changes its mind once it is sanctioned itself.

4.Fine-tuning helps a little.

We fine-tune Qwen3-VL-4B with LoRA and RLOO on 33 norms and test on 17 held-out norms from the same families. Training on compliant actions together with judging other agents’ actions works best, raising joint success from 0.0% to 13.7%. But even after training, the model’s beliefs barely change after it watches other agents, so the main failure remains.

Limitations

NormGym isolates norm learning in a controlled setting and simplifies real social life in several ways. Each episode has a single fixed norm, sanctions are public and immediate, and the other community members follow scripted planners. Real communities have noisy feedback, varied behavior, and several norms that change over time. Our fine-tuning experiments also use only one small base model.
